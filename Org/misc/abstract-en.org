Peer-to-peer file-sharing protocols such as BitTorrent offer scalability and resilience but expose participants to traffic analysis, provide no mechanism to ensure peer authenticity, and remain exploitable by dishonest nodes. This dissertation proposes {{{mark(bqti)}}}, a proof-of-concept BitTorrent client that combines three distinct technologies to mitigate these issues: instead of traditional TCP or uTP, it relies on the QUIC transport protocol for encrypted, multiplexed communication, I2P for traffic anonymity, and {{{gls(tee)}}} remote attestation for hardware-rooted peer authenticity. Similar to blockchains, the client introduces a proof-of-work admission barrier and PGP-rooted swarm tokens to increase Sybil resistance and defend against impersonation attacks, thereby binding the peer's identity to its Kademlia DHT position. By leveraging a Keystone enclave, {{{mark(bqti)}}} runs isolated, trusted code, ensuring that each peer runs an unmodified client implementation, thereby closing the trust-anchor gap that no current BitTorrent mechanism addresses.

Functional and performance evaluation demonstrated that the client is capable of identifying attacks at their protocol boundaries. At the same time, the anonymity layer, despite proxying the underlying QUIC traffic, introduced significant performance overhead, largely due to I2P network characteristics. The results showed that hardware-rooted trust can be integrated into anonymous P2P file-sharing while remaining architecturally sound. Meanwhile, keeping a modular design in which each layer is a self-contained plugin that meets the user's security and performance needs.
