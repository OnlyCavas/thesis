* Conclusion

:CUSTOM_ID: conclusion
:END:

Peer-to-peer networks provide decentralised intercommunication, offering better scalability and resilience than centralised alternatives by relying on a community of peers rather than a single provider, thereby freeing up resources and enabling them to propagate across the network through peer-to-peer sharing. However, these networks expose participants to traffic analysis, lack mechanisms to verify peer authenticity, and remain vulnerable to incentive exploitation, in which dishonest peers consume others' resources without contributing back to the network. This thesis addressed these weaknesses by implementing a proof-of-concept ({{{mark(bqti)}}}) that unifies three complementary technologies: QUIC as the encrypted transport, I2P as an anonymity layer, and TEE-based remote attestation for hardware-rooted peer authenticity. It was investigated whether these technologies could be combined into a single, coherent, trust-enhanced file-sharing system, using BitTorrent as a representative P2P file-sharing protocol.

The initial premise of the research was the use of *Onion Routing* as the anonymity layer, reflecting an early assumption that Tor-style routing would be a natural choice for a distributed file-sharing protocol. However, as described in Section {{{ref(tor_vs_i2p)}}}, this thesis concluded that I2P is significantly better suited to P2P applications, as the Tor Project explicitly discourages BitTorrent traffic over Tor. The anonymity component of the research question is therefore addressed using a garlic-based network, motivated by the comparative analysis of the two overlays. Addressing the research question, /Can TEE-based attestation combined with QUIC and onion routing meaningfully improve security and anonymity in P2P file-sharing without unacceptable performance penalties?/, meaning whether TEE attestation, along with QUIC and garlic routing, complement each other without significantly impacting functionality, and still maintain an acceptable performance despite appending more layers.

Security-wise, several mechanisms were implemented to promote fairness, discourage dishonest behaviour, and mitigate common P2P attacks. The proof-of-work admission requirement, quantified over $10{,}000$ iterations (see Section {{{ref(eval_sn1)}}}), imposes a computational barrier that renders large-scale Sybil identity generation infeasible, with the difficulty parameter dictating the cost per identity. This requirement is reinforced by a PGP-signed CA root that delegates trust to each swarm, defining a swarm as a self-governed Darknet, and by hardware-bound trust levels that distinguish between attested and software-only peers. Together, they mitigate the attack vectors evaluated in Section {{{ref(eval_sn2)}}}, *token forgery*, *replay*, *and piece substitution*, all of which were detected and contained. The addition of TEE remote attestation enforces hardware-rooted proof that participants are running unmodified clients, thereby establishing inherent trust among peers and enabling participants' client versions to be subject to a swarm's admission policy, as proposed in future work (see Section {{{ref(fw_protocol_reputation)}}}).

At the anonymity layer, the proof-of-concept successfully integrated with the I2P network by proxying QUIC traffic through the overlay, maintaining the same internal interface and transparently carrying the complete protocol stack: *admission*, *peer discovery*, *attestation*, and *piece exchange*. Respecting client modularity allows I2P to operate on top of the standard QUIC transport, thereby requiring no changes to the protocol stack implementation.

Despite successfully integrating the three technologies into a single BitTorrent client, adding the anonymity overlay comes with a significant performance penalty. Owing to the I2P network characteristics along with the current implementation design, a significant overhead was observed (see Section {{{ref(eval_sn3)}}}): sustained throughput decreased from approximately {{{mark(104.27 MB/s)}}} over a direct QUIC transport to roughly 80 KB/s when routed through I2P, for a {{{mark(10 MB)}}} payload. This worsened with a larger {{{mark(1.5 GB)}}} payload, producing an unsteady connection with recurrent bursts of 10 to 20 pieces separated by approximately one minute and occasionally severing the connection upon I2P tunnel rebuilds, making long download sessions impractical, taking up to several hours to be completed.

This thesis produced a functioning QUIC-native BitTorrent client that successfully replaces the traditional TCP and uTP transport protocols entirely, multiplexing protocol traffic, such as *DHT*, *PEX*, and *piece exchange*, over a single QUIC connection per peer, including a simplified tit-for-tat choking algorithm. Additionally, it extended Kademlia DHT with a proof-of-work admission barrier, PGP-rooted swarm tokens that allow each swarm to maintain its own self-governed trust domain, and a rate limiter that mitigates DoS attacks. Notably, it extended the admission procedure by relying on a remote attestation report mechanism built around Keystone, binding the node's hardware identity directly to its DHT position and anchoring its DHT actions and piece-level integrity verification to that identity. Rather than relying on a centralised provider for remote attestation, it proposed a developer-maintained signed manifest that lists the enclave hashes corresponding to its {{{mark(bqti)}}} version. Finally, it offers a modular I2P anonymity layer that complements the underlying QUIC protocol, transparently proxying its traffic through the I2P network. Together, these contributions demonstrate that these technologies can coexist and complement one another to create a BitTorrent client offering hardware-rooted trust within an anonymous, incentive-aware swarm.

Naturally, as a proof-of-concept and given the time constraints of a single MSc project, this work has limitations that should be addressed in future work to create a fully feature-complete BitTorrent client. First, the TEE integration was developed and evaluated within an emulated Keystone RISC-V environment rather than on physical secure hardware, with support for a single TEE backend, but designed to accommodate other backends, such as *OP-TEE*. Additionally, the anonymity layer, although functionally complete, creates significant overhead, rendering large downloads impractical. To address this issue, it is proposed to replace the SAM bridge with an embedded I2P router and a custom congestion controller tuned for I2P network characteristics (see Section {{{ref(fw_protocol_opt)}}}). Finally, the evaluation was conducted on a two-machine testbed rather than on a large live swarm, thereby leaving scalability behaviour uncharacterised. Nonetheless, the trust primitives developed throughout this thesis could provide the necessary properties to keep evolving this concept, by integrating alongside the DHT a full reputation system (see [[#fw_protocol_reputation][Section 6.2]]) that extends the current trust level metadata, leveraging the hardware-rooted attested identity, and ultimately progressing towards {{{mark(bqti-FS)}}}, a zero-trust distributed filesystem concept (see Section {{{ref(fw_zt_fs_bqti)}}}).

Lastly, this thesis addresses a long-standing weakness of BitTorrent and P2P systems in general: the inability of peers to guarantee that a counterpart is an honest participant running a verified client. By relying on hardware-rooted attestation and combining it with encrypted transport and anonymous routing, this work demonstrates that closing this gap is not only feasible but also architecturally sound. Despite routing traffic through the anonymity overlay significantly impacting performance, the choice remains entirely with the user.
