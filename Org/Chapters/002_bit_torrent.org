** BitTorrent Protocol Fundamentals

Since its introduction in 2001, BitTorrent has grown into one of the most widely deployed peer-to-peer protocols, having historically accounted for a significant fraction of the global internet traffic {{{citep(izal_2004)}}}. Its decentralised nature removes the need for a single origin server, distributing both content and transfer load across all participating peers, improving scalability and availability. As a result, it presents itself as a robust content-sharing solution that relies on a sparse community of peers rather than a single centralised entity.

Decentralised networks offer resilience and scalability, making them a foundational technology across domains ranging from file-sharing distribution to telecommunications. As open-source systems such as Matrix {{{citep(matrix)}}} adopt similar decentralised principles, they may be keen to offer a more user-controlled software option rather than the standard, proprietary options.

In this section, the BitTorrent protocol architecture is examined, along with its operational mechanics, and common attack vectors that compromise both network integrity and contributor anonymity.

{{{gls(bt)}}} is one of the most widely used technologies for P2P (Peer-to-Peer) content distribution networks. According to the BitTorrent Protocol specification {{{citep(bt_001)}}}, it presents an advantage over plain HTTP, where multiple downloads can happen concurrently for the same file, safeguarding against one HTTP server handling multiple requests. In simple terms, peers upload the pieces they already possess to one another, spreading the file across the swarm and distributing the transfer load among them. That said, within an active swarm exist two categories of peers, sustaining the seeder-leecher model, such as:

+ *Downloaders (Leechers)*:

  These nodes have incomplete copies of the file, focusing on downloading the missing pieces to complete it, while serving concurrently already verified pieces to other participants within the swarm. The terminology /leecher/ carries negative connotations, referring to peers who download without adequately uploading in return, violating the reciprocity principles on which BitTorrent is based. This behaviour harms the entire swarm by breaking the balance that sustains the seeder-leecher model, potentially rendering torrents incomplete or unavailable to new peers.

+ *Seeders (Uploaders)*:

  These nodes hold a complete copy of the file and are solely responsible for keeping the torrent alive. At least one seeder must always be present, distributing as many pieces as possible. Without it, newcomers cannot complete a full download, as missing pieces will remain unavailable. Therefore, within a swarm, a torrent without a seeder is considered unavailable or dead, making seeders critical to maintaining the swarm's health and longevity.
