For others to exchange chunks of files with peers, it's paramount that they communicate with each other. One of the most used mechanisms for Peer discovery is centralised *trackers*.

*** Trackers
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: trackers
:END:

These are *centralised dedicated servers* that neither *store* nor *transfer any file* resources. Instead, they focus on maintaining a list of active *peers* in each swarm. At any given time, a client can request a list of new peers from a *tracker* that will give it a random subset of *IP addresses* of other participants on the swarm, that may have what it is looking for.

Despite being a crucial *entity* for peers discovering themselves and thus connecting to the swarm, their use can raise privacy concerns. As *trackers* keep a record of all peers active in the swarm, they log their *IP Address* and later link their actions to them, de-anonymising them.

The centralised nature of trackers introduces significant scalability limitations. As the network grows, trackers must scale accordingly to aggregate the increasing number of both files and new remote peers joining the swarm. {{{cite(bt_pex_003)}}} reports that at the time, one of the most popular BitTorrent networks, The Pirate Bay, was experiencing repeated TCP timeouts under high load. Apart from that, centralised trackers represent a Single Point of Failure, which by nature are susceptible to DoS attacks that can weaken or entirely take down a swarm.

*** Distributed Hash Table (DHT)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: distributed_hash_tables
:END:

Nowadays, modern *BitTorrent* has evolved into robust, decentralised alternatives that aim to mitigate these vulnerabilities and reduce or eliminate reliance on trackers. In this case, a peer can use the same *.torrent* manifest file to locate bootstrapping nodes and connect to the Mainline Distributed Hash Table.

A Distributed Hash Table is a simple key-value database distributed across a network of interconnected computers, called nodes. All nodes belong to the *Computer Space* and are assigned a key that defines their position in the *Key Space*, where both nodes and values are defined.

Among several {{{gls(dht)}}} designs, Kademlia is one of the most widely adopted {{{citep(kademlia_2002)}}}, and BitTorrent DHT Mainline is Kademlia-based. By definition, it exposes four RPC operations running over UDP: {{{mark(STORE)}}}, {{{mark(FIND\_NODE)}}}, {{{mark(FIND\_VALUE)}}} and {{{mark(PING)}}}. These operations collectively enable peer discovery, data storage, and retrieval across the distributed network. Each node maintains a routing table that maps the key space and divides it into k-buckets, grouping the nearest peers by their XOR distance from the local node ID. By default, node and value keys are 160-bit (20-byte) identifiers, consistent with the SHA-1 hash space used by the common BitTorrent client.

Upon joining the DHT, a client must complete an admission procedure to gain access to the swarm by querying a bootstrapping node. According to the authors of the paper, the two most widely deployed implementations are the Azureus DHT and the Mainline DHT {{{citep(bt_pex_003)}}}. However, Mainline DHT is the most widely used implementation among major clients, such as uTorrent, BitComet, and Mainline.

Relying on bootstrapping nodes instead of centralised trackers removes the Single Point of Failure, by allowing the network to scale naturally with peer growth, eliminating the bottleneck introduced by tracker-dependent peer discovery and significantly reducing the impact of DoS attacks.

*** Peer Exchange (PEX)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: peer_exchange
:END:

Once a node has established P2P connections with swarm participants using either the previous peer-discovery mechanisms, it can gossip with the neighbours to expedite their routing table updates, within specified time frames. This process relies on a P2P exchange of a small, real-time set of *active peers* and *dropped peers*, using a fast request-response model to update each node's internal routing table quickly, gossiping possible known peers with compatible nodes that implement a version of the {{{gls(pex)}}} protocol. Doing this promotes decentralisation and further improves upon the {{{gls(dht)}}} bootstrapping method, with peers completing admission and using gossip techniques to foster network synchronisation. According to the authors of this paper {{{cite(bt_pex_003)}}}, some of the widely used PEX protocol implementations that clients used are: *{{{latex(AZ\_PEX)}}}*, used by *Azureus Clients*, *{{{latex(UT\_PEX)}}}*, which are used mainly by *uTorrent*, *qTorrent*, etc. and *{{{latex(BC\_PEX)}}}* by *BitComet* clients.

Evaluating PEX performance is paramount to understanding how it impacts the overall performance of a BitTorrent client.{{{cite(bt_pex_003)}}} evaluates PEX, which assembles a testing environment to effectively test the performance impact of also relying on a gossip protocol, using 100 randomly selected torrents of different sizes, acquired from *Pirate Bay* and *Mininova*.

Throughout the experiment, the authors observed a significant decrease in data transfer time on PEX-enabled testbeds, reporting a mean speedup of 0.07, corresponding to a download time reduction of about 7%, concluding that PEX can effectively improve the client's connection speed and thus reduce download time, thereby creating a relationship between download speed and neighbour size.

Furthermore, the authors found that by using PEX gossip, a client can establish 25 concurrent connections to other participants at startup. In contrast, without PEX, the number of connections drops to only 5. Despite these benefits, BitTorrent's choking algorithm means that the more connections it establishes, the greater the likelihood that it will be choked by others, reducing the number of successful outgoing connections. The experiment observed that, once a client reached peak connection count, outgoing connections dropped to half after the download completed. On the other hand, if the client accumulated enough pieces to offer in return, it could engage in reciprocal exchange with additional peers, gradually improving the overall download rate.

Finally, the authors observed that a client would accumulate a larger number of IP addresses, only moments after the initial tracker connection. Thus, decreasing the number of requests to a tracker and gathering the necessary neighbours to allow the node to quickly incorporate into the swarm, directly improving its *search ratio* relative to the number of known peers. In summary, the authors acknowledge that the PEX gossip protocol helps accumulate more IP addresses after admission, directly translating into faster admissions and download rates, and affecting the percentage of active seeders.
