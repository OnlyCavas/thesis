* Introduction
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: introduction
:END:

Peer-to-peer (P2P) networks are a cornerstone of modern Internet infrastructure, enabling decentralized interaction between  hosts while remaining scalable. Unlike traditional client-server architecture, P2P networks allow each participant to act as both client and server, thus exchanging data without relying on a centralized source. This approach has been adopted across a wide range of applications, including distributed file-sharing, blockchain networks, online gaming. As a result, P2P traffic constitutes a significant portion of today's global Internet traffic, offering greater resilience to single points of failure, improved fault tolerance, and better scalability compared to traditional centralized servers.

Among the most popular P2P protocols, BitTorrent stands out as one of the most used solutions. It is particularly well-suited for distributing large files, typically outperforming centralized alternatives by removing the bottleneck of a single origin server. As a result, it has been adopted across open-source software distribution, academic dataset sharing, content delivery, and most notably, large-scale unofficial content distribution.

However, despite its adoption and efficiency, BitTorrent and other similar P2P protocols suffer from critical security and privacy vulnerabilities that can jeopardize user safety and information. These vulnerabilities expose participants to malicious peers who may distribute harmful content disguised as legitimate files. The protocol is susceptible to traffic analysis, exposing the participant IP address and making them vulnerable to de-anonymization attacks and surveillance. Furthermore, the ecosystem relies on unverified client implementations that could contain malicious code or vulnerabilities and other security issues, more critically, peers possess no mechanism to authenticate one another, leaving the swarm open to infiltration by malicious nodes. While the distributed nature of the network provides resilience and fault tolerance, it still exposes peer identities. The network relies on an implicit trust model difficult to enforce, enabling adversarial peers to operate with relative impunity and undermining the integrity of the public swarm.

Several solutions partially mitigate these issues, such as protocol encryption (MSE/PE) or VPN tunnelling, that offer partial protection. However, protocol encryption primarily addresses ISP throttling rather than peer authentication or meaningful anonymity. Usually, VPNs shift trust to third-party providers, most of the time keeping record of their activities. Additionally, services such as VPNs are vulnerable to de-anonymization in cases where providers cooperate with third parties or are compromised without the user's knowledge, and they often impact performance significantly. Furthermore, anonymity networks such as Tor explicitly discourage BitTorrent traffic due to the high bandwidth usage. Most concerning, no viable mechanism exists to verify whether connecting peers are trustworthy or whether they may jeopardize others on the network.

This thesis explores a novel approach to addressing these stated security and privacy challenges in P2P file-sharing through the integration of three complementary technologies: *Trusted Execution Environment (TEE)* based client attestation, the *QUIC* transport protocol, and onion routing techniques. By combining secure hardware, modern network transport security, and traffic anonymization, this research investigates whether meaningful security and anonymity guarantees can be achieved without compromising overall performance.

** Motivation
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: motivation
:END:

This thesis proposes a proof-of-concept BitTorrent Protocol that aims to mitigate these challenges by combining three complementary mechanisms:

  + *TEE based Client Attestation*: Leveraging Trusted Execution Environments solutions provided by hardware platforms such as *Intel SGX/TDX*, *ARM TrustZone*, or *AMD SEV-SNP*, this mechanism enables peers to cryptographically attest to the authenticity and integrity of their client implementation. For example, when a peer joins the swarm, it can prove to the other participants that it is running a known, unmodified client implementation within a secure enclave. This approach prevents peers from accepting connections from compromised or malicious clients, thereby significantly reducing the attack surface against content poisoning, protocol manipulation, and other attacks related to P2P. Unlike approaches that depend on trusting third-party certificates authorities or centralized identity providers, this mechanism roots trust directly in hardware, improving overall security guarantees.

  + *QUIC*: The QUIC protocol (Quick UDP Internet Connections), the transport foundation of HTTP/3, can provide many advantages over BitTorrent's current TCP based transport. QUIC offers mandatory encryption by default, reducing passive eavesdropping opportunities. Its multiplexed stream architecture eliminates head-of-line blocking, improving download efficiency when receiving pieces from several peers in parallel. This thesis will also focus on ways to implement QUIC as the native transport layer for both peer discovery mechanisms (DHT and PEX) and chunk exchange inside the swarm, improving performance and security while reducing implementation complexity.

  + *Anonymity techniques*: To address traffic analysis and IP exposure vulnerabilities, this research evaluates the integration of onion routing techniques such as *I2P*. Rather than connecting to peers and exposing IP address to all swarm participants, connections are routed through multiple garlic relays, with each hop knowing only the next node. This approach also addresses the exit node problem, where the last node remains susceptible to traffic analysis. This approach aims to prevent:

    + *Traffic Analysis*: Adversaries attempt to correlate network traffic patterns between entry and exit nodes to infer user behaviour and de-anonymize their activities within the network.

    + *IP Leakage*: Malicious entry nodes or network intermediaries may log connection metadata. For instance, compromised Tor entry nodes can record client IP addresses, while BitTorrent trackers maintain logs of peer addresses requesting to join swarms.

    + *De-Anonymization Attacks*: Adversaries correlate user activity across multiple networks or sessions through traffic fingerprinting and timing analysis, enabling identification of users despite anonymization efforts.

  This research investigates the feasibility of integrating onion routing into the BitTorrent protocol while keeping in mind critical performance trade-offs. Specifically, analysing the bandwidth overhead introduced by relay nodes, latency introduced by multi-hop routing, and the inherent tension between anonymity guarantees and practical usability for end users.

The main challenge addressed by this thesis is the inherent tension between security, anonymity, and performance in P2P systems. Each protective measure introduced carries additional computational and network overhead. TEE attestation requires cryptographic operations and trusted hardware availability. QUIC encryption introduces additional processing overhead. Finally, onion routing inevitably increases bandwidth consumption and end-to-end latency.

This study aims to evaluate how these technologies interact when integrated into the BitTorrent protocol, proposing a proof-of-concept secure file-sharing protocol that mitigates the vulnerabilities described earlier. In doing so, the research addresses the balance between security, anonymity, efficiency, and practical usability.

#+begin_quote
/Can TEE-based attestation combined with QUIC and onion routing meaningfully improve security and anonymity in P2P file-sharing without unacceptable performance penalties?/
#+end_quote

** Objectives
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: objectives
:END:

  To investigate further the research question, this thesis defines the following objectives:

    + *Design and implement a DHT admission protocol*: Develop a Kademlia-based peer discovery and admission protocol incorporating proof-of-work Sybil resistance and CA-anchored token authentication per swarm, allowing peers to verify the legitimacy of swarm participants.

    + *Integrate QUIC*: Implement QUIC as the underlying transport protocol for both peer discovery and piece exchange, incorporating mutual TLS with a PGP-rooted PKI for swarm-scoped trust, departing from the traditional TCP-based BitTorrent transport.

    + *Implement TEE-based peer attestation*: Develop a remote attestation mechanism, enabling hardware-rooted identity guarantees that provide a higher trust level than software-only solutions.

    + *Integrate I2P anonymity routing*: Route swarm traffic through an anonymity overlay, mitigating traffic analysis and preventing IP exposure.

    + *Evaluate performance trade-offs*: Evaluate security properties under adversarial conditions, validating the implemented security layers against concrete attack vectors, quantifying key performance metrics and demonstrating attack detection and mitigation at their respective protocol boundaries.
