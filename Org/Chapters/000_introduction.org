* Introduction
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: introduction
:END:

Peer-to-peer (P2P) networks enable decentralised communication, removing the single point of failure commonly found in traditional centralised networks. In this paradigm, devices communicate directly with each other, creating a decentralised mesh network of peers that delivers better scalability and reliability, distributes information among themselves, and offers a better persistence model than the centralised alternative.

Among the most popular applications leveraging decentralised peer-to-peer communication are file-sharing networks, blockchain systems and online multiplayer gaming. These types of applications rely on high-bandwidth, low-resource consumption connections, efficiently allocating resources among participants. The most prominent example is BitTorrent, a distributed file-sharing protocol that often outperforms centralised alternatives, offering faster download times for larger files and allocating higher bandwidth than traditional CDN servers, which typically bottleneck or limit bandwidth. BitTorrent is primarily used for open-source software distribution, academic data sharing, content delivery, and the distribution of unofficial copyrighted content.

Despite BitTorrent networks offering scalability and resilience, adversarial nodes exploit their intrinsic characteristics, thereby jeopardising user confidentiality, network availability, and integrity. Exposing participants to malware distribution, traffic analysis, surveillance, free-riding, and swarm poisoning, corrupting honest peers, deceiving them into spreading misinformation to others, and slowly taking over the swarm.

More importantly, current BitTorrent implementations lack a mechanism for peers to authenticate one another and guarantee that the counterpart is using a verified, honest client implementation, thereby preventing dishonest clients from participating. On top of that, accounting for the decentralised nature of BitTorrent, there must be a client capable of masking the user identity, allowing them to hide from surveillance, while enforcing incentives honestly and keeping the swarm scalable, performant, and healthy.

Several solutions offer anonymity by hiding the user behind a publicly known server, such as MSE/PE protocol encryption or VPN tunnelling. However, neither of these solutions offers meaningful anonymity, shifting trust to a third-party provider that may log user activity data while using its service. Third-party providers are known for sharing users' private data or cooperating with other entities, thereby de-anonymising users' network fingerprints and often impacting performance and experience. On the other hand, dedicated anonymity overlays such as Tor avoid this single point of trust, but explicitly discourage BitTorrent traffic due to its bandwidth demands, leaving an uncertain path for anonymous P2P file-sharing.

This thesis explores a novel approach that integrates three complementary technologies: *Trusted Execution Environment (TEE)-based client attestation*, the *QUIC* transport protocol, and a solution for routing QUIC traffic through an anonymity overlay. This thesis implements a custom BitTorrent client that deviates from the standard protocol, aiming to maintain confidentiality, availability, and integrity within a swarm, leveraging trusted hardware for peer authenticity, integrating with an anonymity overlay to hide the client's identity, and experimenting with a modern, increasingly adopted secure transport protocol. This research investigates whether this combination of technologies provides meaningful security and anonymity while safeguarding performance, compatibility, and distribution, supporting a wide range of hardware.

** Motivation
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: motivation
:END:

This thesis proposes a proof-of-concept BitTorrent Protocol that aims to mitigate these challenges by combining three complementary mechanisms:

  + *QUIC*

    Even though traditional BitTorrent clients rely on either the TCP transport protocol or uTP for networking, the main objective of this dissertation is to experiment with new technologies and create a new paradigm. The {{{gls(quic)}}}, the transport layer of HTTP/3, outperforms TCP-based connections by offering mandatory encryption by default, reducing passive eavesdropping. Its multiplexed stream architecture removes head-of-line blocking, enabling concurrent requests to be dispatched over a single connection to a counterpart. This dissertation focuses on implementing peer discovery mechanisms (DHT and PEX) along with piece exchange natively, leveraging QUIC's properties to address TCP constraints.

  + *Anonymity Overlay*

    To prevent traffic analysis and IP exposure, thereby protecting the user's identity, this research evaluates the integration of two anonymity overlays, namely *Tor* and *I2P*. The main objective is to route QUIC's underlying transport traffic through one of these overlays, thereby maintaining QUIC's properties and ensuring users' anonymity, helping to prevent:

      + *Traffic Analysis*:

        Adversaries attempt to correlate network traffic patterns at the network edge, thereby inferring peers' behaviour and profiling their actions within the network.

      + *IP Leakage*:

        Prevents intermediary nodes from logging connection metadata, thereby preventing the compromise of user anonymity. For instance, BitTorrent trackers maintain logs of joining peers' addresses.

    The research investigates the feasibility of integrating an anonymity overlay that proxies the underlying QUIC's traffic while preserving the control and security mechanisms QUIC provides, acknowledging the performance trade-offs involved. Specifically, it analyses the difference between standard and proxied QUIC, comparing the increased latency and reduced bandwidth of the anonymous path against the inherent tension between anonymity and practical usability.

  + *Trusted Hardware (TEE)*:

    Traditional BitTorrent client implementations lack a mechanism to remotely attest to neighbours, proving their authenticity and integrity. To solve this, Trusted Execution Environments such as *Intel SGX/TDX*, *ARM TrustZone*, or *AMD SEV-SNP* enable peers to remotely attest to others that they are running trusted code within a secure enclave. For instance, during swarm admission, a newcomer produces an attestation report signed with the enclave's private key, proving that it is running a known, unmodified client version. If the attestation report is invalid, participants reject incoming connection attempts, declaring the caller as potentially malicious. This approach roots the swarm's trust anchor directly in hardware rather than in software certificate authorities, thereby significantly reducing the attack surface for content poisoning, protocol manipulation, and other common P2P attacks.

This thesis addresses the inherent tension among security, anonymity, and performance in P2P systems, where each protective measure carries additional computational and network overhead. For instance, TEE remote attestation not only requires special hardware but also introduces computational overhead. At the transport layer, QUIC's congestion control and flow control introduce network overhead in high-churn, low-bandwidth networks. Finally, at the anonymity layer, introducing an anonymity overlay inevitably increases latency and reduces effective bandwidth.

This study aims to evaluate how these technologies interact when integrated into the BitTorrent protocol and proposes a proof-of-concept secure file-sharing protocol that mitigates the vulnerabilities described earlier. In doing so, the research addresses the balance between security, anonymity, efficiency, and practical usability.

** Objectives
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: objectives
:END:

  To investigate the research question, this thesis defines the following objectives:

    + *Design and implement a DHT admission protocol*: Develop a Kademlia-based peer discovery and admission protocol that incorporates proof-of-work Sybil resistance and CA-anchored token authentication per swarm, enabling peers to verify the legitimacy of swarm participants.

    + *Integrate QUIC*: Integrate QUIC as the underlying transport protocol for both peer discovery and piece exchange, incorporating mutual TLS with a PGP-rooted PKI for swarm-scoped trust, departing from the traditional TCP-based BitTorrent transport.

    + *Implement TEE-based peer attestation*: Incorporate a remote attestation mechanism that enables TEE-based peers to achieve a higher trust level within the swarm than software-only peers.

    + *Integrate an anonymity overlay*: Find and evaluate the optimal anonymity overlay for proxying QUIC's traffic, mitigating traffic analysis and preventing IP exposure.

    + *Evaluate performance trade-offs*: Evaluate security properties under adversarial conditions, validating the implemented security layers against concrete attack vectors, and demonstrating attack detection and mitigation at their respective protocol boundaries.
