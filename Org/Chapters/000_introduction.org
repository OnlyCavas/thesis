* Introduction
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: introduction
:END:

Peer-to-peer (P2P) networks enable decentralised communication, removing the single point of failure commonly found in traditional centralised networks. In this paradigm, devices communicate directly with each other, creating a decentralised mesh network of peers that delivers better scalability and reliability, distributes information among themselves, and offers a better persistence model than the centralised alternative.

Among the most popular applications leveraging decentralised peer-to-peer communication are file-sharing networks, blockchain systems and online multiplayer gaming. These types of applications rely on high-bandwidth, low-resource consumption connections, efficiently allocating resources among participants. The most prominent example is BitTorrent, a distributed file-sharing protocol that often outperforms centralised alternatives {{{citep(izal_2004)}}}, offering faster download times for larger files and allocating higher bandwidth than traditional CDN servers, which typically bottleneck or limit bandwidth. BitTorrent is primarily used for open-source software distribution, academic data sharing, content delivery, and the distribution of unofficial copyrighted content.

Despite BitTorrent's capabilities, it exposes participants to security and anonymity threats, including malware distribution, traffic analysis, surveillance, free-riding (see {{{ref(free_riding_mechanisms)}}}), and swarm poisoning, and lacks a mechanism to prevent dishonest clients from connecting and exploiting the swarm's resources.

** Motivation
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: motivation
:END:

While current BitTorrent implementations offer scalability and resilience, adversarial nodes exploit their intrinsic characteristics, thereby jeopardising user confidentiality, network availability, and integrity. More importantly, these implementations lack a mechanism for peers to authenticate one another and guarantee that the counterpart is using a verified, honest client implementation, thereby preventing dishonest clients from participating. Given the decentralised nature of BitTorrent, there must be a client capable of masking user identity, allowing users to hide from surveillance, while enforcing incentives honestly and keeping the swarm scalable, performant, and healthy.

Several solutions offer anonymity by hiding the user behind a publicly known server, such as MSE/PE protocol encryption {{{citep(introduction_mse)}}} or VPN tunnelling. However, neither of these solutions offers meaningful anonymity, shifting trust to a third-party provider that may log user activity data while the user is using its service. Third-party providers are known for sharing users' private data {{{citep(introduction_vpn)}}} or cooperating with other entities, thereby de-anonymising users' network fingerprints and often impacting performance and experience. On the other hand, dedicated anonymity overlays such as Tor avoid this single point of trust, but explicitly discourage BitTorrent traffic due to its bandwidth demands {{{citep(tor_blog_project)}}}, leaving an uncertain path for anonymous P2P file-sharing.

Thus, there is currently no BitTorrent specification that reconciles all three requirements: peer authenticity and integrity, network-level anonymity, and acceptable performance. Existing approaches address these requirements only in isolation.

** Research Questions
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: research_questions
:END:

This thesis defines the following research questions:

  + *RQ1*: How can peer authenticity be established within a decentralised swarm, thus detecting dishonest or modified clients without relying on a centralised authority?

  + *RQ2*: Can QUIC act as the sole transport protocol, sustaining peer discovery and piece exchange, thus replacing completely TCP or uTP?

  + *RQ3*: How can a BitTorrent client's traffic be anonymised while preserving the underlying transport's protocol properties, such as stream multiplexing and congestion control?

  + *RQ4*: How can TEE-based hardware be integrated within the peer authentication procedure, and what performance penalty does it impose alongside the anonymity overlay?

** Objectives
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: objectives
:END:

To answer the defined research questions, this thesis proposes the following objectives:

    + *Design and implement a DHT admission protocol*: Develop a Kademlia-based peer discovery and admission protocol that incorporates proof-of-work Sybil resistance and CA-anchored token authentication per swarm, enabling peers to verify the legitimacy of swarm participants. /(RQ1)/

    + *Integrate QUIC*: Integrate QUIC as the underlying transport protocol for both peer discovery and piece exchange, incorporating mutual TLS with a PGP-rooted PKI for swarm-scoped trust, departing from the traditional TCP-based BitTorrent transport. /(RQ2)/

    + *Implement TEE-based peer attestation*: Incorporate a remote attestation mechanism that enables TEE-based peers to achieve a higher trust level within the swarm than software-only peers. /(RQ1 and RQ4)/

    + *Integrate an anonymity overlay*: Find and evaluate the optimal anonymity overlay for proxying QUIC's traffic, mitigating traffic analysis and preventing IP exposure. /(RQ3)/

    + *Evaluate performance trade-offs*: Evaluate security properties under adversarial conditions, validating the implemented security layers against concrete attack vectors, and demonstrating attack detection and mitigation at their respective protocol boundaries. /(RQ4)/

** Contributions
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: contributions
:END:

Relative to the work reviewed in Chapter {{{ref(sota)}}}, this thesis makes the following contributions:

+ QUIC as the sole transport protocol

  Unlike all surveyed BitTorrent clients, which rely on either TCP or uTP, this thesis proposes a Rust BitTorrent client implementation that replaces the underlying transport, multiplexing DHT, PEX, and piece-exchange traffic over a single QUIC connection per peer.

+ Mutual TEE attestation upon bootstrapping

  Taking into consideration solutions such as BITE {{{citep(tee_009)}}} and SafeKeeper {{{citep(tee_safekeeper)}}}, which rely on TEE remote attestation to prove that sensitive code is running on a valid, isolated environment (enclave), but depend on a centralised verification service (Intel's IAS) to validate attestation reports, this thesis implementation leverages hardware-rooted attestation (Keystone/RISC-V) to prove to a bootstrapping node that the initiator is running a verified, honest client implementation, thereby being granted a higher trust level than software-only peers. Rather than relying on a centralised component such as these solutions, the client is bound by its enclave hash, published in a signed manifest that any participant can verify independently.

+ Variable Peer identity

  Unlike solutions such as Tribler {{{citep(pouwelse2008tribler)}}}, which derive a single static peer identifier from the public key and use it as the peer's swarm identity for communication and reputation, this thesis client implementation generates a fresh certificate derived from the asymmetric key pair generated upon startup that serves as the peer's swarm identity only in software-only mode, changing upon every DHT integration. In TEE-supported peers, the enclave's public key is used as the DHT identity.

+ Proxy QUIC's traffic over I2P

  This thesis sustains a modular design by allowing both direct QUIC and QUIC over I2P connections, supporting I2P via a SAM bridge abstracting the anonymity overlay and maintaining the same underlying behaviour without modifying the transport layer, thereby preserving QUIC's security and management features such as multiplexing and congestion control, keeping full client functionality in both modes.

+ Empirical evaluation of both client performance and security

  A testbed was defined to evaluate the performance and security of the client's implemented layers against token forgery, replay, and piece-substitution attacks, and to quantify the overhead between direct QUIC and I2P-routed QUIC downloads in a local testing environment.
