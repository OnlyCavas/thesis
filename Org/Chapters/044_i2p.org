*** Garlic Routing (I2P Project)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: garlic_i2p_routing
:END:

In 2003, the I2P (Invisible Internet Project) was introduced {{{cite(i2p_garlic)}}}, an early evolution of other anonymous networking projects that drew inspiration from *Freenet* and early *Tor* concepts. Unlike *Tor*, it implements *Garlic Routing*, offering a more decentralised alternative to *Onion Routing*. Similarly to Tor, establishing a tunnel requires a three-hop connection, where the *garlic message* is encrypted several times with symmetric keys negotiated with tunnel peers. The message is later sent through the inbound tunnel, and at each node, it removes a layer of encryption until it arrives at the inbound gateway. Additionally, the *garlic message* is bundled with multiple application messages.

I2P is essentially a P2P {{{citep(i2p_005)}}} network where every node is both client and server, and it uses a distributed database that stores two types of metadata, such as:

+ *RouterInfos*: Contains contract information about the I2P participant, including its capacity, IP address, port, and its key.

+ *LeaseSet*: Contains the inbound tunnel information for a destination, enabling other nodes to route messages to it.

Unlike *Onion Routing*, which establishes a bidirectional tunnel with a three-hop circuit traversing inbound and outbound messages, *I2P* instead creates a pool of four distinct, unidirectional tunnels: two inbound and two outbound. I2P supports two tunnel types: *exploration tunnels*, with 0 to 2 hops, for querying NetDB to discover new peers or publishing LeaseSets, and *client tunnels*, with 3 to 7 hops, tied to a specific I2P application and destination, carrying high-priority information, such as user data or application data {{{cite(i2p_007)}}}. During communication between two peers, eight tunnels are created in total: two inbound and two outbound, with one exploratory and one client tunnel in each direction. Unlike *Tor*, which provides access to the ClearNet, I2P operates as a darknet, routing traffic inwards rather than outwards. However, I2P supports outproxies, volunteer nodes that route traffic from the I2P network to the ClearNet, but it is not recommended and may weaken anonymity guarantees that I2P offers.

To establish a connection between two peers, the initiating peer advertises its *inbound gateway* and LeaseSet by publishing them to the {{{gls(netdb)}}}. A group of special Floodfill routers maintains this database {{{citep(i2p_005)}}}, collectively storing a distributed hash table of peers and hidden services. Each DHT entry is identified by a 32-byte *routing key* calculated by hashing the concatenation of the destination's identifier and the current UTC date string using SHA-256 {{{citep(i2p_005)}}}. The key rotates daily at UTC+0, limiting any DHT poisoning attacks to a 24-hour window. A volunteer can manually configure a Floodfill router or have it automatically promoted based on its bandwidth capacity after successfully passing stability and uptime stress tests.

After publishing its LeaseSet, the router queries the NetDB to find the destination's LeaseSet, obtaining the metadata needed to reach the destination gateways. The router then generates a secret value (nonce) and sends it to the destination's inbound gateway. Upon receiving the nonce, it forwards it to the initiator's inbound gateway. After receiving the nonce from its outbound tunnel, if both nonces match, the handshake is completed. By including its own LeaseSet in the reply, the destination allows the initiator to skip the NetDB query, reducing latency and minimising metadata leakage.

While I2P is designed as a darknet, it is possible to proxy traffic to the ClearnNet via outproxies {{{cite(vs_008)}}}. An outproxy, similar to an onion exit node, routes garlic traffic from within the network to the ClearNet. Still, its use is discouraged due to the same issues as with exit nodes, and the potential to de-anonymise users. Additionally, outproxy maintainers bear significant legal risk, as their IP address is associated with all traffic routed through them, making them liable for other users' actions.
